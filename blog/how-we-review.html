How we review live TV services

A useful review shows its test conditions, evidence and limits. Here is the process we will use before writing about a named provider.

Who’s behind this blog: LoopMint publishes these articles and sells viewing plans. We have a commercial interest in this market, and we’ll disclose any known referral, affiliate, supplier or other relationship when we review a service.

We haven’t rated any named provider yet. Before we do, we’ll need to test the service and show what we checked, where we checked it and what we still couldn’t confirm. This is how we plan to do that.

1. Record what we actually tested

We’ll name the website, test date, country, device and player, internet connection, and whether we used a trial or paid plan. If a screenshot helps show a feature, we’ll hide account details. Reading a sales page is useful research, but it isn’t the same as using the service.

We’ll separate what we observed from what the provider told us and anything we couldn’t verify. If we can’t get into the service, we may describe its public website, but we won’t pretend we tested playback or support.

2. Check country and content claims

Before testing, we’ll ask which countries the provider serves and what was available in the place we tested. A broadcaster, sports league or film logo doesn’t prove that a service has access to that content. If the provider makes a licensing claim, we’ll say whether we checked it independently or are reporting the provider’s own statement.

We’ll also explain what the offer is: live channels, on-demand viewing, a player app, or some combination. “IPTV” on its own doesn’t tell you what a plan includes. The app and the viewing service can be separate products with separate costs.

3. Test the customer experience

We’ll note how long signup takes, how the login details arrive and what it takes to get the player running. During the test, we’ll try the menus, startup, picture, sound, programme information and subtitles where available. We’ll also say when we tested and what the connection was like, so readers know how much to read into the result.

For device support, we’ll name the model or operating system whenever we can. If we haven’t tested a TV, we won’t repeat a broad claim like “works on every Smart TV” as if we had.

4. Read pricing and support terms

We’ll compare the price on the site with the amount a customer is asked to pay. A review should spell out the plan length, simultaneous-screen limit, renewal, trial and refund terms, and any separate app fees. We’ll ask support a real setup question and describe the answer, when it arrived and where we sent it. A quick reply is nice; a helpful one matters more.

5. Publish limits and corrections

Every review should cover what worked, what didn’t and what we still don’t know. We’ll date meaningful updates and correct mistakes when better evidence comes in. We won’t invent testimonials or measurements, or copy a provider’s marketing. If we recommend a service, we’ll explain who it might suit and what we based that on.
